Recently, I’ve started using Claude Code and Codex much more often for development, both at work and personally. Or rather, it’s almost entirely AI-driven development now.

Especially since the beginning of last year, my personal trust in AI-generated code has increased considerably.



Of course, mistakes still happen. But I feel that I no longer approach it the way I used to:

“Since this code was written by AI, I should read it while questioning everything first.”



At work this week, I found myself increasingly feeling that the review process was wasteful.

At Unimal, the company I belong to, we have an AI knowledge-sharing session, and I talked about this topic there.

I then looked into several papers to understand the current situation.

## Google isn’t saying that humans can be replaced completely either

When thinking about this topic, research on Google’s code reviews was particularly useful.

Google’s AutoCommenter uses LLMs to review coding practices that are difficult for traditional linters to evaluate.

The research found that AI was able to cover a considerable portion of the best practices that human reviewers frequently point out.

On the other hand, what I found interesting about this Google research is that **they aren’t aiming to point out as many things as possible**.

Apparently, even a small number of false positives can make developers think, “This bot is saying something unnecessary again,” causing them to lose trust in it.

As a result, they place considerably more emphasis on **not making unnecessary comments** than on coverage.

This direction was also quite close to what we had been feeling through our own development work.

## What perspectives should humans focus on?

Something I realized through conversations with people inside and outside the company is that the focus of what humans review is gradually shifting outward from the code itself.

For example:

- Is our understanding of the requirements correct in the first place?
- Should this change really be introduced?
- Could it affect other functionality?
- Does it feel natural from a UI/UX perspective?
- Could it cause problems in operations?
- How should we explain it to customers?
- Is this risk acceptable?

These are the kinds of things I mean.

Frontend work in particular is ultimately used by humans, so people need to review it from a UX perspective as well.

The overwhelming majority opinion was that even if something is correct as code, it is meaningless if it is difficult to use.

Going forward, I think we need to look not at **whether the code is correct**, but at **whether the change as a whole is correct**.

## Redefining what we call “review”

AI writes the code, another AI reviews it, and if there is a problem, AI fixes it as is.

If that is the case, the need for humans to go back and forth multiple times saying “Please fix this” and “I fixed it” becomes smaller than it used to be.

I thought that was certainly true.

If AI is responsible for everything from implementation through fixes, there is absolutely no need for humans to carry review comments back and forth along the way.

Thinking about it that way, I felt that the term **Code Review** itself may gradually become mismatched with the way we develop software today.

Perhaps what humans do is closer to reviewing the change and granting permission for it.

In other words, it is not about judging:

“Did the person who wrote this code write it correctly?”

but rather:

“Is it okay to introduce this change into the system?”

## If so, the meaning of Approve also changes

Until now, I think Approve has carried a strong meaning of “I read the code and confirmed that there were no problems.”

When adding “LGTM++; 🚀”, I might also include that I read the code and checked that it did not seem likely to cause errors, as well as the areas that might be affected.

However, as AI starts writing large amounts of code, it is becoming increasingly difficult to operate under a process where humans read every piece of generated code in detail.

That is why I think the meaning of Approve will also shift toward **the person who approves the change taking responsibility for the decision to merge it**.

The review method itself can be anything. We can have AI review something as many times as we want, or increase the number of automated tests.

But in the end, someone will still be needed to decide, “This is good enough to introduce.”

At least, that is how I see it for now.

## I also want AI to decide whether human review is necessary

Something I want to try going forward is having AI make the judgment of **whether a particular change requires human review**.

For example, classify changes into something like:

- Low
- Medium
- High

A wording change would be Low.

Changes to authentication, billing, or infrastructure would be High.

UI changes would require human confirmation.

If AI could make judgments like these, humans could focus on important reviews.

That said, I am still afraid to leave this entirely to AI at 100%.

Google’s research also showed that loss of trust caused by false positives is a significant problem in AI reviews.

So at first, I think it would be fine to have AI assign labels while **humans review everything as well**.

Then record things such as:

- A human found a problem even though the change was classified as Low
- The change was classified as High, but in reality it did not need to be reviewed
- What kinds of problems the AI review missed

Once enough results have accumulated and we learn that “this type of change is almost always fine,” we can reduce human review only within that scope. I think we can gradually build up a track record and expand our trust (a design pattern for accumulating virtue).

## Review perspectives become an organizational asset

Another thing I realized was quite important is the review perspectives we provide to AI.

AI has become considerably smarter, but it will not review something perfectly just because we say, “Review this appropriately.”

Previously, we also discussed internally that “It will perform an analysis if you tell it to do something that sounds like a review, but if you don’t tell it, it will write that kind of code.” That is still very much the case.

So we should define perspectives such as:

- Requirements fulfillment
- Security
- Correctness
- Scope of impact
- UI/UX
- Operations
- Testing

Furthermore, if AI misses something, **add it to the review perspectives for future reviews.**

We should continue improving the review criteria.

Until now, much of the know-how behind code reviews has existed inside the heads of experienced engineers.

If we can put that know-how into words and give it to AI, the know-how itself becomes an organizational asset.

## Code review as education is likely to change too

I think code review has served not only as a quality check but also an educational purpose.

It was similar to pair programming: a senior engineer would look at a junior engineer’s code and teach them, “It would be better to write this like this.”

However, AI has started providing considerable assistance with the act of writing code itself.

If so, what we need to confirm for educational purposes will be less “Why did you write it this way?” and more:

**“Why did you make this decision?”**

- Why did you choose this implementation?
- What other approaches were available?
- Why did you accept this risk?
- How far did you think the impact would extend?

I feel that being able to explain these kinds of things will be more important for engineers going forward.

## Look at the change itself rather than who wrote it

There was another study that I found useful.

It was a code review experiment involving 447 engineers at an organization where the use of AI by Microsoft had become commonplace.

Even for the same code, no effect on evaluations was observed from displaying that “AI was used.”

On the other hand, when the code was labeled as having been written by a **junior engineer**, there appeared to be a tendency for its evaluation to decline even though the code was the same.

I thought this was a fairly interesting result.

Going forward, perhaps reviews should be conducted not by thinking, “I’ll look at this carefully because of who wrote it,” but rather:

**“I’ll look at this carefully because the change carries a high risk.”**

Of course, there will still be times when things need to be explained in detail from an educational perspective. But for members who have basically joined the project, it will likely become important to treat them as engineers in their own right.

## I’ll try this for a while

For now, I am thinking of a flow like this:

```
AI implements
    ↓
Another AI reviews from multiple perspectives
    ↓
AI makes fixes
    ↓
Another AI performs a second review
    ↓
The need for human review is assessed
    ↓
Humans review the requirements, impact, UI/UX, and operations
    ↓
A human approves
```

I am not trying to eliminate human review immediately.

Rather, **I want to spend human time on the areas where human review adds value.**

If the role of writing code is changing, then the role of reviewing code should change as well.

I’ll try operating this way in an actual project for a while and think about it again.
