# In an Era When AI Writes Code, What Will Engineers Do? —— What I Talked About at Sakurajima House 2026

On October 18, 2026, I spoke at Sakurajima House 2026.

This time, the theme was “What does it mean to be an engineer in an era when anyone can generate code?”

I talked with GMO Pepabo's Director and CTO, Kentaro Kuribayashi (Anchipo-san), about engineering in the age of AI.

Originally, three of us were scheduled to speak, but Matsuoka-san was unable to attend due to poor health, so the session suddenly became a two-person session.

I am the VPoE at Unimal, and recently, AI-assisted development has been progressing considerably within the company as well.

As a result, development speed has increased, which is great, but different problems from those we had before have started to emerge. (I feel like I wrote some details about this in an earlier article.)

For example, how much do we need to understand about code written by AI? How should we review the things that AI now allows us to produce in large quantities? How should we evaluate people who are achieving results using AI?

This time, I brought those kinds of everyday concerns to Anchipo-san.

As we talked, I realized that perhaps my very way of understanding the problems had been slightly off.

I want to write down some of those thoughts before I forget them.

## Do We Need to Understand All the Code Written by AI?

The first topic we discussed was whether humans need to understand code generated by AI.

At Unimal, since we started using AI, the number of Pull Requests has increased by roughly five to ten times compared with before.

It is great that we can produce more, but if humans review all of it, the reviewers ultimately become the bottleneck.

Do we really need to understand all the code generated by AI?

I thought that we needed to understand the specifications and expected behavior, but I had some doubts about whether we needed to grasp every detail of the implementation.

### Who Thinks of It as Code in the First Place?

When we discussed this, Anchipo-san brought up the idea that we should question the premise itself.

We casually say that “AI generates code.”

But perhaps the reason we recognize it as code is that we are engineers.

For example, if you ask someone who created an application using AI, “What language did you write it in?” they might answer, “I don’t know. The AI made it.”

Certainly, from that person’s perspective, it does not matter what programming language was used.

They asked the AI to do something, and something that works was created.

That is all there is to it.

Because we are engineers, we know that there is code behind it. But from the perspective of the person who created it, it does not even need to be code in the first place.

I thought, “That’s true. I hadn’t considered it that way.”

### My Thinking Is Close to AI = Compiler

Personally, I think of the arrival of AI in parallel with compilers.

For example, when we normally write programs, it is not as though we check all the assembly generated by the compiler.

How the CPU executes instructions and how registers are used.

You can create many programs without knowing those things. Nor do you debug the CPU using specialized debuggers called ICE Circuits or JTAG.

Of course, that kind of knowledge may be necessary when investigating low-level problems.

Actually, I quite like these kinds of low-level topics.

Recently, I have enjoyed looking at execution results on CPUs with SVE2 support.

But when it comes to building ordinary business applications, you do not really need to know that much, do you?

I think AI may become something similar.

However, unlike a compiler, AI is nondeterministic, so there is the problem that outputs are not uniquely determined for a given input. Strictly speaking, they are not the same because they are classified under P/NP problems, but from the perspective of an external observer, as in the Chinese room, I feel that AI = compiler.

### Responsibility Matters More Than Understanding

At this point, Anchipo-san made an observation that left an impression on me.

The important thing may not be whether you understand the code, but whether you take responsibility for the result of executing it.

That is certainly true.

Even if you understood all the code, problems can still occur depending on the execution environment.

When that happens, you cannot say, “I understood the code, so it’s not my fault.”

Conversely, even if you do not understand every detail of the code, you may still be able to fulfill your responsibility if you can confirm that it operates appropriately and respond when problems occur.

Until now, I had been thinking in terms of how much of the code we should make people understand.

But perhaps what we should really be thinking about is **what kind of mechanisms would allow us to take responsibility for the systems that are generated**.

## From Now On, We Will Build “Systems That Build Systems”

This was the part of the discussion that left the strongest impression on me personally.

Anchipo-san suggested that the work of engineers in the future may become building “systems that build systems.”

Why do we write code in the first place?

I think it is not because writing code itself is the goal, but because we want to accomplish something.

There is input, processing, and output.

As a result, some kind of value is delivered to users.

Until now, humans wrote code to achieve that.

But if AI has begun writing the code for us, then what humans should do will change.

Rather than writing individual pieces of code, it will be about **creating mechanisms that can continuously build the required systems at an appropriate level of quality**.

In other words, the image is something like building a factory that produces systems.

### AI Does Not Necessarily Produce the Same Result Every Time

What concerned me here was the nondeterministic nature of AI.

With an ordinary program, the same input and conditions will basically produce the same result.

But AI is different.

Yesterday, it implemented something beautifully, but today, for some reason, it writes strange code.

When developing with AI, it is perfectly normal to see things like, “The output from Opus today is somehow different from the output from Opus yesterday.”

Can we really build a factory for creating systems using something so unstable?

The response to this question was an idea similar to an error budget.

Rather than assuming that everything must be built 100% correctly, we decide in advance how much failure we are willing to tolerate.

We classify things according to the magnitude of the risk and reliably stop anything that cannot be tolerated.

The discussion was that we may need mechanisms like that.

When I heard this, I was reminded of CPU and GPU manufacturing.

In semiconductor factories, it is not guaranteed that everything produced will operate normally.

They assume that a certain amount of defects will occur, then inspect the products and sort them according to performance.

This is the so-called yield issue. A well-known example is that when producing products such as Intel’s Core i9/i7, there will inevitably be a certain probability of a yield in which some cores do not work.

Software development using AI may become more like this as well.

Rather than expecting everything generated to be correct, we generate it, verify it, and fix it if there is a problem.

The important thing is **not to eliminate failure entirely, but to detect failures appropriately and prevent problematic things from being released**.

In a sense, current development using AI agents is already doing something similar.

Communicate the goal, break down the work, execute it, and check the results.

How should we design that entire process?

I think these aspects will become increasingly important in engineering going forward.

## If We Can Build Things 10 Times Faster, Does That Really Mean 10 Times the Value?

The next topic we discussed was the increase in development speed brought about by AI.

Recently, I often see people saying that AI allows you to develop ten times faster.

But Anchipo-san brought up the question, “What exactly have we become able to produce ten times more of?”

Can we write ten times as much code?

Can we create ten times as many Pull Requests?

Can we release ten times as many features?

Or has the value we can deliver to users become ten times greater?

Indeed, these are all different things.

Even if we become able to create ten times as many features, it is meaningless if we are simply producing large quantities of things that users do not use.

It could even have a negative impact by increasing the amount of things that need to be maintained.

Being able to produce something and being able to create value are separate things.

### Hadn’t We Been Wasting Human Time in the First Place?

What particularly left an impression on me in this discussion was the observation that we only became conscious of waste after we started using AI.

With AI, the number of tokens and the usage fees appear as figures.

That makes it easy to notice, “Are we really spending this much money?”

But when humans were doing similar work before, were we really that conscious of it?

Humans spend hours reviewing.

They make corrections over and over.

They investigate, try things, and then make more corrections.

That is also a cost, of course.

What is more, human time is limited.

AI’s computing resources can be increased to some extent by paying more money, but human time cannot be increased that easily.

Are we using more valuable human time in order to save on AI usage fees?

This made me think quite a bit.

Of course, that does not mean we can use AI without limit.

But rather than simply looking at the cost of AI, I think we need to consider, including human time, which option can create more value.

## If Someone Achieves Results Using AI, Are Those Results Their Own Ability?

This was a topic I had been quite concerned about as a VPoE.

For example, suppose a third-year engineer becomes highly proficient with AI and continues producing excellent results.

Meanwhile, it is also possible that a senior engineer with many years of experience is unable to produce similar results.

How should we evaluate them in that situation?

Anchipo-san viewed ability as “the capacity to consistently produce reproducible results.”

Whether someone uses AI or anything else, if they can continuously produce the required results, then that is their ability.

I strongly agree with this way of thinking.

I think it is slightly misguided to say that the results are not the person’s own ability because they used AI.

We would not say that a program is not the programmer’s own ability because it was created using a compiler, would we?

AI is also just one tool.

However, it is important not merely to succeed once by chance, but to be able to continuously produce the required results.

## If AI Does Everything, Is There Any Need to Learn Technology?

The final topic was the value of learning technology.

These days, if you ask AI, you can create a reasonably convincing web application without knowing much about programming at all.

So do people who want to become engineers in the future need to study technology?

I still think they do.

Anchipo-san also said that although AI may reduce the opportunities for humans to do engineering, engineering itself will not disappear.

Take security, for example.

Even if AI starts writing the code, the need for memory protection will not change.

An unauthorized process must not be allowed to read from or write to other memory regions at will.

Fundamental principles like these will not change just because AI has appeared.

## So, What Will Engineers Do After All?

Through this session, my thoughts became a little more organized.

Until now, I had been thinking about how humans should respond when AI began generating large quantities of code.

Reviews cannot keep up.

AI costs are increasing.

We do not know how much of the code we should understand.

But after hearing this discussion, I thought that perhaps what we should be thinking about in the first place was slightly different.

Rather than whether humans need to understand all the code, how can we take responsibility for the results?

Rather than the fact that AI allows us to produce ten times as much, what should we be increasing tenfold?

Rather than how much we are spending on AI, how much value are we creating as a result?

And rather than simply whether we can use AI, can we use it to produce reproducible results?

When I think about it this way, I believe that AI is not so much eliminating engineers’ jobs as changing the subject of engineering.

From creating code itself to creating mechanisms that produce systems.

Of course, not all development will immediately become like this.

At present, AI output can be unstable, quality assurance can be difficult, and there are still many challenges.

I am also experimenting every day with how to operate AI agents and how to control CI costs.

However, there is no doubt that the arrival of AI has brought us to a time when we need to reconsider development processes and evaluation systems that we had previously taken for granted.

And what made me think the most this time, personally, was **whether we are wasting human time before worrying about the cost of AI**.

Rather than increasing the number of reviewers simply because the volume of development has increased, we should think about mechanisms that can ensure quality without requiring humans to review everything in the first place.

Rather than making AI do huge amounts of work, we should think about the work that humans truly need to do.

I want to focus a little more of my attention in that direction.

There are still many things for which I do not have answers, but this discussion was extremely helpful in thinking about the development organizations of the future.

Thank you, Anchipo-san, and everyone involved in organizing Sakurajima House 2026!
