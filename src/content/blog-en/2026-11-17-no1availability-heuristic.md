## Availability Heuristic

[https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0010028573900339](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0010028573900339)

- Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases
- Amos Tversky, Daniel Kahneman, et al.
- *Science*, Vol. 185, No. 4157, pp. 1124–1131

## Overview

```
Much research in recent years has been concerned with the validity and consistency of judgments of frequency and probability. However, little is known about the psychological mechanisms by which people assess the frequency of classes or the likelihood of events. [1]
We propose that when people are faced with the difficult task of judging probability or frequency, they use a limited number of heuristics, which reduce these judgments to simpler ones. In another paper, we analyzed in detail one such heuristic, that of “representativeness.” According to this heuristic, an event is judged probable to the extent that it is representative of the essential features of the population or of the generating process. Evidence for representativeness has been obtained in several studies. For example, the majority of respondents without training in statistics believe that the sequence HTTHTH is more likely to result from a coin toss than HHHHTH or HHHTTT, although, of course, all three sequences are equally probable. The sequence judged most probable is the one that best represents both the proportion in the population (1/2) and the randomness of the process (Kahneman & Tversky, 1972). Similarly, both laymen and sophisticated subjects assess the likelihood that a particular individual will be employed in a specific occupation by the degree to which he represents the stereotype of that occupation (Kahneman & Tversky, 1973). Significant biases due to representativeness have also been found in the judgments of psychologists experienced in the statistics of research (Tversky & Kahneman, 1971). [1, 2]
When judging the probability of an event by representativeness, people compare the essential features of the event with the features of the structure from which it is generated. In this way, people estimate probability by assessing similarity, or inclusional distance. Alternatively, people may estimate probability by assessing “availability,” that is, associative distance. From lifelong experience, we have learned that instances of large classes are recalled more readily and more quickly than instances of less frequent classes, that likely events are easier to imagine than unlikely events, and that the associative connection between two events is strengthened when they frequently occur together. Hence, people can estimate the number of instances in a class, the likelihood of an event, or the frequency of co-occurrences by assessing how easily they can carry out the relevant mental operations of retrieval, construction, or association. [1]
For example, one may recall instances of divorce among one’s acquaintances in order to assess the divorce rate in a community, think of the various ways in which a politician could lose support in order to assess the probability that he will lose an election, or assess the strength of the association between violence and predatory animals in order to estimate the probability that a violent person will see a “predatory animal” in a Rorschach inkblot. In all these cases, the estimation of the frequency of a class or the probability of an event is mediated by the assessment of availability. It may be said that a person employs the “availability heuristic” whenever he estimates the frequency or probability by the ease with which instances or associations come to mind. It is not necessary to actually perform the retrieval or construction operations in order to assess availability. It is sufficient to assess how easily these operations can be performed, just as one can assess the difficulty of a puzzle or a mathematical problem without actually thinking of a specific solution. [1]
The strengthening of associative connections by repetition is probably the oldest law of memory known to man. The availability heuristic exploits the converse form of this law: it uses the strength of association as a basis for judgments of frequency. In this theory, availability is not a “dependent variable,” as it is typically treated in memory research, but a “mediating variable.” In general, availability is an ecologically valid cue for judgments of frequency, because frequent events are usually easier to recall or imagine than rare events. However, availability is also affected by a variety of factors unrelated to actual frequency. If the availability heuristic is applied, such factors will affect the perceived frequency of classes or the subjective probability of events. As a result, the use of the availability heuristic leads to systematic biases. [1, 2]
In this paper, we explore the availability heuristic through a series of 10 studies. First, we demonstrate that people can assess availability with reasonable speed and accuracy (Section II). We then show that the judged frequency of classes is biased by availability in the construction of instances (Section III) and by availability in retrieval (Section IV). The empirical studies in this paper deal with judgments of frequency, or with judgments of probability that can be readily reduced to relative frequency. The effects of availability on judged probabilities of essentially one-time events, which cannot be reduced to relative frequency, are discussed in Section V (the final section). [1]
```

## Interesting points

### Huristics = reduce these judgments to simpler ones.

- One of the paper’s proposals
- When people are faced with the difficult task of judging probability or frequency, they use a limited number of “heuristics” that reduce these judgments to simpler ones.
- In other words, simplifying the problem. Convenience.

### The Rorschach test example

- [https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%AD%E3%83%BC%E3%83%AB%E3%82%B7%E3%83%A3%E3%83%83%E3%83%8F%E3%83%BB%E3%83%86%E3%82%B9%E3%83%88](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%AD%E3%83%BC%E3%83%AB%E3%82%B7%E3%83%A3%E3%83%83%E3%83%8F%E3%83%BB%E3%83%86%E3%82%B9%E3%83%88)
- A personality assessment test devised by a psychiatrist in 1921
- A method of estimating thought processes and their disturbances by asking subjects to verbalize what they see in sheets of paper made symmetrical by dripping ink onto them and folding them open
- In 1969, Loren J. Chapman / Jean P. Chapman (the Chapman couple) suggested that the availability heuristic also influenced this test.
  - They conducted a questionnaire on the theme of diagnostic signs of homosexuality, in which many people identified certain responses as signs useful for diagnosis.
  - [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4896551/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4896551/)
  - The results showed the same correlation among 32 clinical psychologists and approximately 600 university students.
  - Specifically, they associated symbols relating to sexual intercourse with diagnostic signs of homosexuality.
- Several years later, in 1972, Golding, S. L., & Rorer, L. G. demonstrated that the availability heuristic was not eliminated even when prior training had been provided.
  - [https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1973-08925-001](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1973-08925-001)
