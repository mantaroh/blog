Today was the major seminar held once every six months.

In graduate school, a seminar is held once every six months, where professors serving as the secondary and primary supervisors discuss your current research with you.

Today, the research direction came together nicely, making it a fruitful major seminar.

However, I did receive a warning from the perspective of a researcher.

## You must not leave “communicating to others” entirely to AI

This may seem obvious, but I would like to explain why I received this criticism, including the circumstances that led to it.



## **Examples of AI Agents Used in Research**

At this point, I cannot imagine conducting research without using AI agents. I think it would be equivalent to being told to conduct research with one arm tied behind your back.

Naturally, I also make extensive use of AI agents in my research. In particular, I leave most of the following tasks to AI agents.

- Paper surveys
- Experiment preparation and dataset collection
- Implementation of code for verifying experimental data

I will explain each of these in detail.



### Paper Surveys

A great many papers related to Web content, which is my area of research, are now being published using LLMs and AI agents.

In the past, I used a brute-force, human-powered approach: picking up relevant terms in Scholar, translating them, and searching through the results. This ended up taking up a large portion of my research. However, as the number of things AI agents can do has increased, most of the work involved in conducting surveys is no longer something people need to do.


|  | Conventional survey | AI agent survey |
| ----- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Search | Registering keywords in Google Scholar | Searching the Internet while taking context into account |
| Investigating relevance | Judging from the context after translation | Extracting only relevant papers at the search stage |
| Credibility | Investigating the conference where it was presented | Instructing the agent to assess credibility at the search stage |
| Close reading | Reading it as-is! | Reading it as-is! |
| Organization | Saving relevant papers to the lab Wiki and also recording them in Notion | Having the agent summarize key points and record them in Notion |


There are other phases as well, but I have only picked out the overview here.

I built this agent using Hermes, but partway through, the ChatGPT service produced better results, so as of September 2026, I leave this work to ChatGPT.

In the past, I would also pick up unrelated papers while investigating their relevance, so the survey workload has decreased considerably. However, unrelated papers sometimes led me to expand my research into cognitive science, so I feel that the breadth of my knowledge has decreased somewhat.



### Experiment Preparation and Dataset Collection

As with surveys, I often use agents for experiments as well.

I proceed carefully with experiment design while bouncing ideas off AI. In particular, there is a possibility that I am biased, so I use this process to have my assumptions challenged.

From the design stage onward, I make extensive use of agents. I mainly turn the following tasks over to agents.

- Implementing the collection of datasets for experiments
- Implementing the frameworks used in experiments



As I was writing this, I realized that most of the preparation for experiments in my research consists of implementing dataset collection and implementing the target programs used in the experiments.

As a result, I leave all implementation related to conducting experiments to agents.

I mainly use Claude Code, but I also use Codex because of token constraints. However, for the past month, I have used only Claude Code.



I was also going to write about verifying experimental results, but I will omit it since it involves the same kind of implementation as experiment preparation.



### I Even Used an Agent for Reporting Experimental Results. And Then

When you use AI agents regularly, they will also summarize the results of your experiments, so I had started using agents for reports as well.

I had AI agents write the reports, then converted them into HTML / PDF files that were easy for me to read and understand.

And then I committed the taboo of having an agent write the foundation of the presentation materials I would use for the seminar.



### The Problem, Put Simply

To put it simply, I think the problem this time was that I treated the materials for understanding something myself and the materials for communicating something to others as the same thing.

Giving the experimental results to an agent and having it organize the information was extremely convenient. It also produced comprehensive and useful material for me to look back on.

However, I regret that I used this material as the basis for my presentation.

AI agents neatly organize everything that has been written. For example, suppose I give an agent the information that “in this experiment, the Web pages generated by generative AI contained W3C violations, and the problems were concentrated around ○○.”  
The agent will then turn it into a presentation format consisting of the background, objective, experimental procedure, results, discussion, and future work.

What is worse, it produces a result without the incompleteness of a human and with no omissions.

At that point, human bias produces a false positive: “I made a presentation that is pretty easy to understand.” In other words, I mistakenly thought it was complete.

The problem here is that this bias was self-contained. I had failed to include the perspective of a reviewer.

The people listening to a presentation have a different context, so it is only natural that things I consider common knowledge may not be common knowledge to them.

I think the problem this time was that I had overlooked that perspective. Even at the seminar, the question I was asked was one that I had already resolved in my own mind: “Wouldn't it be enough to provide everything in the prompt from the beginning?” This question came just before the results in the presentation.

For the people listening, the background explaining why I had come to conduct this experiment was not clear, so I should have explained that background as well.

By the time I asked the agent to compile the material, I think I had already outsourced not only the compilation but also the thought process of what I wanted to communicate to the audience. That is the point I need to reflect on this time.



### Creating Materials and the Task of Communicating to Others

Until now, I had thought of understanding the research, creating the materials, and presenting it as one continuous series of tasks. Then AI agents entered the process.

```
Understanding the research
↓
AI agent
↓
Creating presentation materials
↓
Giving the presentation myself
```

I felt that this process was not significantly different from before, but in reality, it created a major difference.

```
Understanding the research
↓
Deciding what I want the audience to understand and what kind of reaction I want them to have
↓
Deciding the order needed to achieve that
↓
Creating presentation materials
```

I think these steps actually existed. When creating the materials, I imagined myself giving the presentation, while also considering the audience's reactions as I spoke.

In other words, the tasks of creating presentation materials and communicating with the audience overlapped considerably and were nearly the same task. By entrusting this entirely to an AI agent, I effectively placed the seminar—a setting where I wanted the audience to respond—far from my own responsibility.

### This Is Not About Saying That You Must Not Have AI Create Your Materials

As I write this, I do not want this to become the extreme conclusion that it is wrong to leave things to AI.

On the contrary, even after reflecting on what happened this time, I still think it would be wrong to return to an era without AI agents.

I will actively use them for creating outlines and diagrams, proofreading text, checking for omissions, and bouncing around anticipated questions. However, **a human must always think about who they want to understand what**.

This task, which could also be called defining the audience, will become nothing more than meaningless time spent creating empty content unless the actual person giving the presentation decides it.

When you use AI agents extensively on a regular basis, the boundary becomes unclear: what humans should do and what should be left to AI agents.

I also think this boundary will continue to shift more and more in the future, and that we will need to keep dividing the work with agents while constantly thinking about it. That is what I felt from this seminar.

At least for now, it seems that the task of deciding “what to communicate to others” is one that should remain with humans.



### Afterword

I was truly reminded of how imperfect my thinking is.
