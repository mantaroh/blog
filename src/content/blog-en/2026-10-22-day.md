### The Problem of a Distributed Workforce

At Unimal, where I currently work, Kagoshima is our main location, but people also work from Fukuoka, Miyazaki, Kanazawa, Brazil, and various other places.

Even in Kagoshima, many people work online, so we rarely have opportunities for everyone to meet in person. For that reason, one day a month is designated as “Kamukamu Everybody.”

On this day, everyone gathers in the same place as much as possible and works on things outside their usual duties.

Normally, we might spend the whole day finishing one of our own products together, or hold a hackathon.

Recently, we have also been planning many things along the lines of AI x ○○.

### A Week When the Internet Was in Chaos

Many vulnerabilities were announced from last weekend through this week.

- **PHP CVE-2026-109618 / CVE-2026-109619**
- **Node.js CVE-2026-105482 and others**
- **PwnEscape CVE-2026-99999** 

I will not explain each one in detail, but several were highly relevant to our own products and the development work we have been involved in. Their impact was also serious, so we needed to respond quickly.

In the middle of all this, the aforementioned “Kamukamu Everybody” was scheduled to take place this week. So we thought, “Since we have the opportunity, why not spend the whole day as Security Day, identifying and addressing vulnerabilities?” And that is how we all ended up working on vulnerability remediation.

### Targets

It is not the case that everything needs to be addressed across the board; whether a vulnerability applies depends on the environment in which each product runs.

For example, in the case of PwnEscape, the issue is that an attack originating from a guest VM can break through the boundary of the host’s hypervisor. Therefore, measures on the host side are more important than measures on instances running on AWS or GCP.

For that reason, we first determine which vulnerabilities need to be examined this time, then begin by investigating each project to see whether it is affected.

We have more than 200 repositories, and going through them one by one would be overwhelming. So, using AI, we created a tool for finding repository vulnerabilities based on trufflehog, a secret-detection tool. (We cannot make it public because its contents are not sufficiently general-purpose.)

As a result, based on information such as advisories, we narrowed down the list to approximately 50 repositories that needed to be addressed.

The candidates included cases where we needed to check the cloud provider’s response status, as well as cases where we might need to update packages and redeploy on our side. Since it was difficult for one person to check the entire production environment configuration, we first compiled them into a list of candidates for investigation.

We then assigned priorities to that list. This time, we divided the priorities into the following three categories:

- P1: Cases where Docker images or the host machine itself include packages containing vulnerabilities
- P2: Cases running in the cloud on vulnerable services (e.g., AWS Lambda / GCP Cloud Run)
- P3: Cases applicable to development or staging environments

### Visualization / Gamification

Once the list was ready, the next phase was to rely on good old-fashioned human effort, with everyone dividing up the work to investigate and address the issues.

However, our CEO took it a step further by visualizing and gamifying the process.

The screen below is a mock-up of the data from the visualization and is not the actual thing, but, like this screen, it visualizes which repositories currently have vulnerabilities and how many vulnerabilities each person has addressed.

![image.png](/images/image-1.png)

This instantly made it possible to work in a game-like atmosphere.

On top of that, prizes were provided for the top three finishers. We were given quite a bit of incentive to clear the game.

### Results in Practice

To give you the conclusion first, after doing this for a day, we finished addressing the issues in many repositories. That said, new vulnerabilities continue to appear one after another, so who knows what the future holds.

There were also cases that depended on the response status of other services, so for those, we were able to implement mitigation measures such as using a WAF or access restrictions.

However, because completion required deployment to be finished, people whose tasks involved items that took a long time to deploy or waiting for a cloud provider to respond were unable to place highly in the rankings. If we do this again, it looks like we will need to review the conditions.
